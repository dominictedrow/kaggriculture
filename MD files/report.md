# Field Report: the Top-10 Leaderboard

A pattern analysis across 992+ downloaded episodes spanning the ten highest-ranked public Kaggriculture submissions — opening structure, tile layout, hiring cadence, hand movement, selling behavior, and how each strategy answers a collapsing market.

- Source data: `kaggle_replays/playbooks.json` (generated by `kaggle_replays/extract_playbooks.py`) plus a targeted per-turn hand-movement extraction over 6 full-season replays (2 per strategy family).
- Unit of analysis: per-turn `(observation, action)` pairs from `kaggle_replays/replays/*.json`.

## Read this before anything else

**Only three strategies are actually represented here, not ten.** Seven of the ten leaderboard names — Khanh, vladee, Hamed Seyed-allaei, Filip Strzałka, JALKARNA GAUTAM, Yusuke Hayashi, and Furious Monk — run a byte-identical tile footprint at every checkpoint day, an identical hire schedule turn-for-turn, and (per the movement extraction below) identical per-hand-slot roles down to the exact tile counts. That's not convergent design, that's one agent (likely a forked public notebook) submitted under seven names. Below, this group is called **Family C** and treated as one data point; its seven win rates (0.80–0.92 across 82–150 episodes each) are reported anyway because the spread is itself a finding — see §9.

**カワシギ** and **MD. Nazmus Sakib Anik** (**Family A**) aren't byte-identical but share the same opening allocation, the same hire-count sequence day-for-day, and near-identical tile maps through day 20 — almost certainly the same lineage with minor divergence.

**researchstudio.site** (**Family B**) is the one genuinely independent design in the field, and it's also the weakest (0.59 win rate vs. 0.80–0.92 for the other two families).

## 2. Opening structures

*(day 0–5)*

All three families reach the same opening beat by day 1: **plant every available tile in wheat and melon immediately, build both pasture types on day 1, and hold pasture empty for one day before stocking it with the first cow and sheep.** None of the top 10 wastes day 0 — wheat and melon seed purchases and the first plantings land inside the first 24 turns in every family.

**Convergent opening move:** every family front-loads **melon** alongside wheat from turn one, despite melon having zero town demand and the most glut-fragile price curve in the game (see §7). At day 1 the split runs 12 melon / 7 wheat (Family A), 9 melon / 7 wheat (Family B), 5 melon / 5 wheat (Family C). Melon's high base price clearly wins the opening land-allocation decision before any of the three has produced enough volume to hit the glut — the price collapse is a mid-game problem these agents solve by *exiting* melon (§7), not by avoiding it at the start.

### Day-1 tile mix by family

| Family | Melon | Wheat | Sheep | Cow | Empty |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| A — カワシギ / Nazmus | 9–12 | 7 | 2 | 2 | 2–3 |
| B — researchstudio | 9 | 7 | 2 | 2 | 3 (+2 idle pasture) |
| C — Khanh family | 5 | 5 | 4 | 1 | 10 |

Family C's day-1 animal mix is the outlier: 4 sheep to 1 cow, versus an even 2:2 split for A and B. Combined with C reaching the highest peak hand count of any family (14, §4), this reads as a deliberate wool-led early economy rather than a balanced-livestock one — wool's `above_target=3.2` glut sensitivity is milder than melon's, so leaning on sheep early is a safer premium-good bet than the shared melon exposure everyone takes.

## 4. Hired-hand acquisition & movement

### Acquisition cadence

Hands held at end of day (median across episodes):

| Day | Family A | Family B | Family C |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 5 | 5 | 4 |
| 8 | 11 | 9 | 6 |
| 20 (peak) | 12 | 13 | 14 |

**Fast-lean vs. slow-wide:** Family A front-loads — 11 hands by day 8, plateauing near 12 for the rest of the season — a smaller, saturated crew running the highest tiles-per-hand ratio in the field (6.0–6.2). Family C ramps almost linearly, only crossing 10 hands around day 12–13, but keeps climbing to a field-high 14 — a wider crew running a lower per-hand ratio (5.21). Both approaches win consistently; Family B sits in between on hire speed but has the *worst* tiles/hand of the three (4.46), meaning its extra hands aren't converting into extra production as efficiently.

**Cash-capped hire days:** only three of the ten submissions ever hit a day where the requested hire count exceeded what the day's starting money could fund: カワシギ (2 days), MD. Nazmus Sakib Anik (2 days), and JALKARNA GAUTAM (1 day, despite sharing Family C's identical blueprint with six siblings that hit zero capped days). JALKARNA's single capped day, in an otherwise byte-identical family, is a clean illustration that final-day economy is opponent-driven, not policy-driven — see §9.

### What hands actually do: fixed roles vs. itinerant labor

Extracted by tracing every hired hand's position and action turn-by-turn across six full-season replays (two per family, ~4,300 hand-turns sampled per replay). Every hand occupies a **slot index** (0 = first hired that game, 1 = second, and so on). Family C runs a genuinely fixed division of labor: the first four slots hired split cleanly into livestock specialists and crop generalists, and that split holds turn-for-turn across every sampled replay — including replays against completely different opponents.

| Family | Role | Slots | Move-action share | Work-action share | Spatial footprint |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| C — Khanh family | Livestock specialist | 0, 3, 4 | 31–36% | 58–66% | tight — slot 4 never leaves x∈[0,8] y∈[0,4] |
| C — Khanh family | Crop generalist | 1, 2, 5–13 | 48–63% | 36–48% | full board, x&y∈[0,9] |
| A — カワシギ / Nazmus | Most-stationary hand | 0 | 43% | 49–50% | full board — no hand gets pinned |
| A — カワシギ / Nazmus | Everyone else | 1–11 | 46–64% | 30–45% | full board |
| B — researchstudio | Most-stationary hand | 3 | 53–56% | 40–43% | full board — no hand gets pinned |
| B — researchstudio | Everyone else | 0–2, 4–12 | 52–63% | 34–43% | full board |

> Family C's best hand spends 31% of its actions moving. Family B's best hand still spends 53%. No hand in Family B ever drops below roughly half its turns just walking.

**Slot 0, 3, and 4 are the same three jobs in every Family C game.** Slot 0 sits in the NE quadrant and works ANIMAL:COW then ANIMAL:SHEEP tiles almost exclusively (present from turn 0, active 690/720 turns). Slot 3 is a dedicated sheep-pasture hand in the NW (91% of its turns in one quadrant, 251 SHEEP-tile actions vs. 61 COW and a handful of crop actions — essentially never leaves the pasture block). Slot 4, hired around day 7, is an even tighter cow specialist: 94% quadrant purity, a bounding box less than half the board, and 294 recorded COW-tile actions against single digits of anything else. These three roles are identical across every Family C replay sampled, including against opponents outside the top 10 (*syouya tobita*, *Lebxgue.*) — the assignment is baked into the policy, not adapted per game. Slots 1, 2, and every hand hired after day 7 roam the entire 10×10 board working whichever crop tile is due, with roughly double the movement share of the three specialists.

**This is the mechanism behind §1's tiles-per-hand gap.** Family C's specialist slots convert 58–66% of their turns directly into WATER/HARVEST/FEED/CARE actions — the rest of the roster still roams, but three of fourteen hands are running at near-zero travel overhead. Family A never produces a hand that stationary; its best slot still spends 43% of its turns just moving, because its checkerboard tile layout (§3) interleaves crops and animals tile-by-tile, so no hand can sit still and only touch one category. Family B is the extreme case: every single hand, across the whole roster, spends over half its actions moving — consistent with it posting the field's worst tiles-per-hand ratio (4.46 vs. 5.21–6.17) despite hiring at a normal pace. Pinning a few hands to a tight, single-purpose zone is a real, measurable lever the strongest family in this dataset is already pulling.

## 5. Crop & animal buying / land purchase patterns

The dataset doesn't retain individual `BUY_SEED`/`BUY_ANIMAL` call records (only the resulting tile-category curves survive aggregation, covered in §2), but the land-purchase log survives at full fidelity — the one large, discrete, badly-timed-if-wrong purchase in the game.

| Family | 2nd quadrant — median turn | 3rd quadrant — median turn | 2nd-quadrant money at turn start |
|---|---|---|---:|
| A — カワシギ | turn 149 (day 6) | turn 265 (day 11) | $109–131 |
| B — researchstudio | turn 145 (day 6) | turn 218 (day 9) | $1,143–1,154 |
| C — Khanh family | turn 161 (day 6) | turn 241 (day 10) | $594–652 |

**Convergent land-buy pattern: retry-until-affordable, not wait-then-buy.** In every family, the 2nd-quadrant `BUY_LAND` action is issued while under-funded 100% of the time it's recorded — every top-10 agent queues the purchase attempt well before it can afford it and keeps re-issuing every turn (the engine silently no-ops an unaffordable order), letting the purchase land the instant funds clear. This is a deliberate, robust idiom, not a bug: it guarantees the land unlocks on the earliest possible turn, with zero risk of a stale one-shot check missing the affordability window. All ten submissions land the 2nd quadrant on day 6, regardless of family — strong convergent evidence that day 6 is close to the theoretical floor for affording it.

Family B is the outlier worth noting: it reaches the 3rd quadrant three days earlier (day 9 vs. day 10–11) and with more money in hand at the 2nd-quadrant purchase ($1,143 vs. under $700 for A and C). That's consistent with its slower, more conservative hiring curve — more cash sits idle rather than going straight into hands, which buys faster land but apparently not enough production advantage to offset the field-low win rate.

## 6. Selling patterns

Every `SELL` call carries the shed level immediately before the sale and the price achieved. That's enough to separate two genuinely different selling philosophies operating on the same shared market.

| Item | Family | Shed level before sale (median) | Price achieved (median) | First → last sell day |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Wheat | A | 2–3 | $45 | 0 → 29 |
| Wheat | B | 18 | $42 | 5 → 29 |
| Wheat | C | 14–15 | $41–42 | 0 → 29 |
| Melon | A | 0 | $178–186 | 10 → 18–21 |
| Melon | B | 6 | $194 | 10 → 28 |
| Melon | C | 0 | $184–210 | 10 → 21–29 |
| Strawberry | A | 6 | $53–157 | 14–15 → 29 |
| Strawberry | B | 4 | $173 | 17 → 29 |
| Strawberry | C | 6–18 | $112–164 | 14–16 → 29 |
| Wool | A | 4 | $148 | 6 → 29 |
| Wool | B | 4 | $199 | 6 → 29 |
| Wool | C | 0–12 | $148–199 | 6 → 28 |

> "Sell little and often" beats "stockpile and dump" on the same shared market — Family A's wheat, sold at a median shed level of 2–3 units, clears a 7–10% higher price than Family B's, sold at a median shed level of 18.

This isn't a coincidence of the price function: the game's shared inventory pool means every large sale moves the price against the seller more than several small ones spread over time. Family A treats wheat as a drip-feed — sell what's in the shed almost every visit. Family B treats it as a stockpile — let it accumulate to a comfortable buffer, then sell in bulk. Family C sits in between (shed median 14–15) and gets in-between pricing. The pattern holds most cleanly on wheat because it's the highest-volume item by far (2,900–28,000 sell events per family across the dataset, dwarfing every other product); on lower-volume premium goods like wool and strawberry the price swings are large enough (driven by the underlying glut dynamics in §7) that the shed-size effect is harder to isolate from market timing alone.

Fertilizer is the second-highest-volume sell item for every family (2,700–29,500 events) — a byproduct of running COLLECT_FERTILIZER on livestock at scale and having more of it than the FERTILIZE action can consume, so the surplus gets liquidated continuously alongside wheat rather than stockpiled.

## 7. Market response & the melon glut

Bucketing every sale's achieved price into 5-day windows shows the same shape in all three families: wheat climbs steadily across the whole season while melon, strawberry, and wool all crater mid-to-late game. This is the shared-inventory glut mechanic playing out exactly as the price function predicts — and every top-10 agent's response to it is the same: **walk away from the tile, don't fight the price.**

| Bucket | Wheat median price | Melon median price |
|---|---:|---:|
| day 0–4 | $28–30 | — |
| day 10–14 | $37–39 | $232–248 |
| day 15–19 | — | $133–165 |
| day 20–24 | $44–46 | $22–140 |
| day 25–29 | $42–47 | — |

Wheat: staple demand absorbs oversupply and price rises through the whole season. Melon: zero town demand means nothing pulls the price back once it drops.

**Universal melon exit:** every family stops planting melon well before day 20 (compare the day-5 and day-20 tile maps in §3: melon holds 5–12 tiles at day 5, 1–2 tiles by day 20). Most last melon sells land between day 18 and day 21; only two of the ten submissions (researchstudio, Hamed Seyed-allaei) keep selling melon into the high 20s, and both are selling at a median price near $22 by that point — essentially clearing out a dead position rather than farming it. Melon's `above_target=3.6` and zero town demand make it a genuine trap for any agent that keeps producing it past the first glut, and every top-10 strategy already knows to get out.

Wool and milk show a different shape — a mid-season dip (day 15–19, roughly half their day 5–9 price) followed by a partial *recovery* into the high 100s/200s by day 25–29 in most families. That recovery is the town shops absorbing accumulated inventory as more of them come online (§8) combined with players throttling volume once the mid-game dip is visible — unlike melon, wool and milk have real shop-side demand pulling them back up, so exiting isn't necessary, just pacing.

## 8. Town shop unlock response

Town shops unlock automatically every 3 days and consume from the shared market passively — there's no player action tied to "a shop unlocked." The closest thing to a measurable response is whether sell volume clusters around the unlock cadence. Bucketing every sell event by `day mod 3` for wheat, melon, wool, milk, and strawberry across all three families gives a near-uniform split — typically 25–40% per bucket, with no family showing a sharp spike aligned to the 3-day interval.

**Null result, and it's informative.** None of the top 10 agents appears to actively watch `town.unlocked_shops` and time sells around it. Selling cadence tracks harvest/growth-cycle timing instead (wheat's 2–4 day yield window shows up more clearly in the mod-3 split than any shop-unlock signal does). Every top submission treats the town as a passive, constant-ish demand floor rather than an event stream to react to.

## 9. Game-to-game strategy variation

Tile-mode confidence — how often the most-common category at a grid position actually occurred, across every episode a family played — is a direct measure of how deterministic each blueprint is turn-for-turn. It stays flat at 1.00 (or drops only to 0.97) through day 10, then degrades steadily toward the season's end.

| Day | Family A confidence | Family C confidence |
|---|---:|---:|
| 5 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 15 | 0.85 | 1.00 |
| 25 | 0.79 | 1.00 |
| 29 | 0.74 | 0.99 |

Family C stays essentially deterministic the entire season; Family A's blueprint loosens meaningfully after day 15.

**Strategies don't adapt to the opponent — but the scoreboard does.** Family C's seven aliases run the literal same policy (100% identical tile maps through day 25) yet post win rates from 0.80 (Khanh, vladee) to 0.92 (JALKARNA GAUTAM), a 12-point spread. Since the policy is provably fixed, that spread is entirely explained by which opponents each alias happened to be matched against across its 82–150 episodes — not by any in-game adaptation. None of the ten submissions shows behavior that reads as opponent-state-aware (no branch conditional on the opponent's public `farms` data, no visible reaction to an opponent's tile footprint or sell volume). That is direct, replay-level confirmation that a genuinely opponent-aware layer would be operating in territory none of the current top 10 touches.

## 10. Late-game liquidation

*(day 25–29)*

Every family runs the same closing sequence, just at different tile counts. Strawberry (and any surviving carrot/melon) gets converted to wheat through days 25–28 — wheat tile counts climb every one of those days in all three families, peaking at day 27–28. Then, on day 29, the field empties: crops get harvested and not replanted, animals stay fed but tile counts of everything else collapse toward EMPTY, and weeds start accumulating for the first time all season as tending stops.

| Family | Wheat tiles, day 27 (peak) | Strawberry, day 27 | Empty, day 29 | Weeds, day 29 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A — カワシギ / Nazmus | 29–31 | 21–22 | 30–34 | 13–16 |
| B — researchstudio | 47 | 8 | 49 | 1 |
| C — Khanh family | 56 | 5 | 31 | 5 |

**Wheat is the exit vehicle, not the strategy.** All three families converge on wheat as the last thing they grow before the season ends — not because wheat is their best crop (strawberry and wool clear far higher per-unit prices, per §6), but because it's the fastest to harvest and liquidate cleanly with no leftover shed inventory or half-grown tiles losing value. Family B pushes this hardest (47 wheat tiles at day 27, strawberry down to just 8) and also shows the cleanest exit — only 1 weed tile at day 29 vs. 13–16 for Family A — suggesting its more conservative, less labor-saturated crew (§4) has slack capacity to fully clear the board at the end, even though that same conservatism costs it across the rest of the season.
